# Gulliver Intro to Business — Lesson Coverage Spec

**Purpose.** This is the canonical list of what each Gulliver lesson covers. It's the
single source of truth that both sides of a lesson are built from:

- **The teaching (Jeff chat)** must explicitly land *every* bullet before the lesson ends.
- **The questions** may only test facts that appear as a bullet here — nothing more.

The bug we're fixing: today the lesson is generated *from the questions'* concept tags, and
for the big lessons (3–6) there are far more concepts than a ~5-message chat can teach, so
questions appear about things the lesson never actually said. Locking coverage here lets us
(a) keep every lesson to a teachable scope, and (b) verify no question is off-spec.

**Convention.** Each bullet is a single, testable idea stated the way a student should be able
to answer it. "Split recommended" flags a lesson whose scope is too big for one chat and should
become several short lessons (the way 1.x and 2.x already are).

## Unit 1 — What a Business Is

### gulliver-1-1 · What a Business Is and What It Sells
- A business is an organization that provides **goods or services** to satisfy customers' wants and needs (the simplest definition).
- A business must **stay useful to customers to survive**; usefulness is what keeps customers coming back.
- The economic term for the usefulness a business creates is **utility**.
- A **good** is a tangible, physical product; a **service** is intangible work/action performed for someone.
- A distinguishing feature of a service: it **can't be stored** — an unbooked service hour is lost forever (unlike an unsold physical good).
- Many businesses **bundle a good with a service** to add value and stand out (e.g., a phone store sells phones *and* repairs screens).
- A business differs from a **hobby**: it exists to serve customers / earn money, not just for personal enjoyment.
- Skill: correctly **classify real examples** as a good, a service, or both.

### gulliver-1-2 · Revenue, Costs, Profit & Loss
- **Revenue** = total money taken in from sales (price × quantity).
- **Costs / expenses** = everything the business pays out (materials, rent, labor, gas, permits).
- **Profit = revenue − costs**; a **loss** is when costs are greater than revenue.
- Revenue and profit are **not the same** — a business can have big revenue and still lose money.
- Skill: **calculate** revenue, profit, or loss from given numbers.
- Two ways to raise profit: **increase revenue** or **cut costs**.
- Comparing two businesses with equal revenue but different costs shows costs decide who "did better."

### gulliver-1-3 · Entrepreneurs, Risk & Reward
- The **entrepreneur** is the person who has the idea, gathers the resources (money, people), and takes the risk.
- The entrepreneur **"gets paid last"** — earns only after workers, suppliers, and other costs are covered, and may earn nothing in a bad month.
- The entrepreneur's main contribution is **organizing the business and taking on its risk**.
- **Risk and reward rise together**; a business generally can't be both perfectly safe *and* hugely profitable for long.
- **Opportunity cost** = the value of the next-best option you give up when you choose one path.
- Entrepreneurs accept risk in exchange for the chance at **reward and ownership**.
- Skill: identify **who is acting as an entrepreneur** in a scenario.

### gulliver-1-4 · Living Standards & Stakeholders
- **Standard of living** = material well-being — the income and goods/services a person can afford.
- **Quality of life** = non-material well-being — time, health, stress, relationships.
- The two can move in **opposite directions** (e.g., high pay but overworked and miserable).
- Skill: recognize cases of **low standard / high quality of life** and vice versa.
- A rising standard of living means people can **afford more goods and services**.
- **Stakeholders** = anyone affected by a business's decisions: customers, workers, owners, community, suppliers.
- Running a business means **balancing competing stakeholder interests**.
- Skill: identify **which stakeholders a specific decision affects**.

### gulliver-1-5 · Outsourcing, Insourcing & Nonprofits
- **Outsourcing** = paying an outside (often overseas) firm to do work instead of doing it yourself; usual motive is **lower cost**.
- A real **downside of outsourcing**: less control over quality, and distance/complexity.
- **Insourcing** = bringing work back in-house; trades higher cost for **more control, quality, and speed**.
- A **nonprofit** reinvests any surplus into its **cause** rather than paying it to owners.
- A nonprofit **still must take in more than it spends** to survive.
- Skill: identify nonprofits and true statements about how they work.

## Unit 2 — The Business Environment

### gulliver-2-1 · The Factors of Production
- The **five factors of production**: land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship, and knowledge/information.
- **Land** = natural resources (farmland, water, oil).
- **Labor** = the workers a company hires.
- **Capital** = money *and* the tools/equipment/machines it buys (business meaning of "capital").
- **Entrepreneurship** = the factor that **organizes the other four and takes the risk**.
- **Knowledge** = skills and know-how (a chef's skill, knowing how to run a shift, a secret recipe).
- Skill: **classify real examples** into the correct factor.
- Money alone isn't enough — a business needs **all** the factors to run.

### gulliver-2-2 · Entrepreneur vs. Employee (Trade-offs)
- The core trade-off: entrepreneurship offers **freedom and higher upside but less security**; a job offers **steady pay but a ceiling**.
- **Upsides** of entrepreneurship: being your own boss, potential big payoff, control over the work.
- **Downsides**: unstable income, more risk and stress, having to **"wear every hat,"** and getting **paid last**.
- The single biggest thing an entrepreneur gives up vs. an employee is a **steady, guaranteed paycheck**.
- Skill: **match a person's preferences** (predictability vs. payoff) to the better fit.
- Skill: identify what is / isn't a genuine trade-off.

### gulliver-2-3 · The Economic & Legal Environment
- A business operates under **rules and conditions it can't control** — its external environment.
- Government/legal actions that **help** business: tax cuts, simpler registration, subsidies, fair courts that enforce contracts.
- Government/legal actions that **hinder** business: heavy taxes/regulation, and **unpredictable rules that change often**.
- The **economic environment** (growth vs. recession, stable vs. wildly swinging prices) shapes whether even a good business succeeds.
- A **shrinking economy** makes even a well-run business struggle (customers spend less).
- Skill: label an example as a government **help** or **hinder**, or as part of the economic/legal environment.

### gulliver-2-4 · Technology & E-commerce
- The **technological environment** = the fast-moving world of tech tools businesses use; it keeps changing what a business *can* do.
- **E-commerce** = buying and selling over the internet.
- E-commerce's biggest gift to a small business: **reach customers anywhere** (before the internet, selling out of state needed physical distribution/middlemen).
- **B2C** = selling directly to everyday consumers; **B2B** = selling to other businesses.
- Whether a business is B2C or B2B **changes how it sells**.
- Skill: classify an example as B2C or B2B.

### gulliver-2-5 · Data & Identity Theft
- A business **database** is its stored collection of information (e.g., customers' names, card numbers).
- A database is a business's **biggest asset and biggest responsibility** — valuable to run the business, and a target for criminals.
- A stolen customer database can lead to **identity theft** because it holds personal/financial data.
- **Identity theft** = someone using your personal information to impersonate you (e.g., open accounts, make charges).
- A company that stores customer data has a **responsibility to protect it**.
- Skill: pick the **strongest habits** for protecting personal information online.

### gulliver-3-1 · How the U.S. Economy Grew Up: Four Eras
- The U.S. economy moved through **four eras in order**: agricultural → industrial → service → information/knowledge.
- **Agricultural era**: most Americans worked as farmers.
- **Industrial era**: most people built goods (cars, steel, appliances) on assembly lines.
- **Service era**: booming demand for services (hospitals, repair shops, restaurants).
- **Information era**: software, data, media, and apps drive most growth.
- Skill: place an economy in the right era from a description.

### gulliver-3-2 · The Forces Pushing on Business Today
- **Competitive environment**: rivals push each other to cut prices and add features — good for customers.
- **Demography** = the study of a population's makeup; a more **diverse** population changes how companies advertise.
- **Aging population**: as a big generation grows old, demand rises for age-related businesses (healthcare, easy-open packaging, larger print).
- **Gen Z** (~mid-1990s to early-2010s): businesses study them closely and market to their **values** (e.g., sustainability).
- **Globalization**: businesses source, produce, and sell **across countries**.
- **Geopolitical risk**: conflict or trade fights between countries can disrupt a business (e.g., delayed shipments).
- **Greening**: a company reducing its environmental impact (e.g., recycled materials, lower pollution).
- Skill: match a force to the business response it drives.

## Unit 4 — Economics & Capitalism  ⚠️ Split recommended

*Currently one lesson covering ~9 concepts. Proposed split:*

### gulliver-4-1 · What Economics Studies
- **Economics** = the study of how people use **limited resources** to satisfy unlimited wants (scarcity is why it exists).
- **Macroeconomics** studies the whole economy (e.g., national unemployment); **microeconomics** studies individual units (one shop, one family's budget).
- Skill: sort a question as **macro vs. micro**.
- **Resource development** = finding better ways to use or create resources.
- Skill: recognize an everyday choice as "doing economics."

### gulliver-4-2 · Capitalism and Its Thinkers
- The **four basic rights of capitalism**: private property, keeping your profits, freedom of choice, and fair competition.
- **Capitalism** = an economy where individuals (not the state) mainly own businesses and resources.
- The **invisible hand** (Adam Smith): people pursuing their own gain unintentionally benefit society (a baker earns money *and* feeds the neighborhood).
- **Adam Smith** is known as the "father of modern economics/capitalism."
- **Thomas Malthus** predicted population would outgrow the food supply — which largely **didn't** happen because of technology/resource development.
- **State capitalism** = the government plays a large role in owning/steering businesses (contrast with private ownership).

## Unit 5 — Markets & Economic Systems  ⚠️ Split recommended

*Currently one lesson covering ~11 concepts. Proposed split into 2–3 short lessons:*

### gulliver-5-1 · Supply, Demand & Market Price
- **Law of demand**: as price goes up, buyers generally want **less**.
- **Law of supply**: a higher price makes sellers want to **supply more**.
- **Equilibrium (market) price** = where supply and demand meet; the price buyers and sellers settle on.
- Skill: predict a price move when supply or demand shifts (viral hit ↑ demand → price up; bumper crop ↑ supply → price down; crop disaster ↓ supply → price up).

### gulliver-5-2 · Market Structures
- **Perfect competition**: many sellers of a nearly identical product; no single seller sets the price (e.g., wheat farmers).
- **Monopolistic competition**: many sellers of **slightly differentiated** products (e.g., clothing brands with their own logos).
- **Oligopoly**: a **few** large firms control most of the market (e.g., major airlines/phone carriers).
- **Monopoly**: **one** seller with no competitors (e.g., the only electric company in town).
- Skill: rank structures by number of sellers (fewest → most).

### gulliver-5-3 · Economic Systems
- **Free markets**: genuine **benefits** (choice, innovation, efficiency) and real **limits** (can ignore fairness, the environment, or people left behind).
- **Socialism**: the government takes a large role in owning/running key industries and redistributing.
- **Communism**: the state owns essentially all production; little/no private ownership.
- **Mixed economy**: a blend of free markets and government involvement — the U.S. is best described as **mixed**.

## Unit 6 — Measuring & Steering the Economy  ⚠️ Split recommended

*Currently one lesson covering ~13 concepts. Proposed split:*

### gulliver-6-1 · The Numbers That Measure an Economy
- **GDP** = the total value of goods and services a country produces; rising GDP over years means the economy is **growing**.
- **Gross output** counts total sales at **every stage** of production, so it's larger than GDP (which counts final value).
- **Unemployment types**: **frictional** (between jobs / new grad searching), **structural** (skills don't match new jobs, e.g., automation), **cyclical** (layoffs from a recession).
- **Inflation** = prices rising across the economy so money buys less; **stagflation** = high inflation *plus* a weak, high-unemployment economy.
- **CPI** tracks the price of a basket of **consumer** goods; **core inflation** leaves out **food and energy** because they're volatile; **PPI** measures prices at the **producer** level.
- **Productivity** = output per worker per hour; rising productivity means more goods per worker.

### gulliver-6-2 · The Business Cycle & Policy Responses
- The **business cycle** phases: expansion (growth, hiring) → peak → contraction/recession (falling GDP, layoffs) → **trough** (lowest point) → recovery.
- **Fiscal policy** = **Congress and the President** using **taxes and government spending** to steer the economy.
- **Monetary policy** = the **Federal Reserve (the Fed)** using **interest rates** to steer the economy (raise rates to cool inflation; lower to boost a weak economy).
- Skill: match "who acts" to "what tool," and label an example as fiscal vs. monetary.
- **John Maynard Keynes** argued government should spend to lift the economy out of a slump.
- **Deficit vs. national debt**: a deficit is one year's shortfall (spending > revenue); the national debt is the **accumulated total** of past deficits.
